3. WHAT IS VREF ADJUSTED AND VSIG ADJUSTED?
Part of the LED light goes straight through the cuvette to the signal detector. Another (smaller) part is reflected to a reference detector.Because the splitter doesn’t divide the light equally (e.g. 20% vs 80%), the two detectors naturally see very different brightness levels. Example: Imagine the LED gives out 100 units of light.The splitter sends 20 units to the reference PD. 80 units pass through the cuvette to the signal PD. If the sample absorbs some light and only 40 units reach the signal PD, the raw readings are:

Reference PD = 20
Signal PD = 40

Now if you just take the ratio (20/40 = 0.5), it looks like the sample absorbed “too much” compared to reality — because the reference PD never had the full 100 units in the first place.So the correction step scales both readings back to what they would have been if each PD had seen 100 units:

Reference adjusted = 20/20×100=100
Signal adjusted = 40/80×100=50
Now the ratio is 100/50 = 2 → correct absorbance.

7. The normal beer lambert law assumes A =εbC.
This assumes your light is just one pure wavelength (like a laser).But in reality, your LED isn’t a single color — it has a range of wavelengths (a spectrum). For example: A “265 nm LED” actually shines from ~255–275 nm. Different wavelengths are absorbed differently by ANT-EPO. So you can’t just treat it as one clean number — you need to average over the whole spectrum.
The integral just means: add up the contributions from every wavelength in the LED spectrum. The integral is the math way of summing up those contributions to get the true absorbance.
